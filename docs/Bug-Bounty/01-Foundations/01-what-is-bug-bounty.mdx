# What Bug Bounty Really Is

`Bug Bounty` · `Stage 1 — Foundations` · `Level: L0 → L1` · `Reading time: ~11 min`

## The Deal in One Sentence

A company publishes a **program** that says: *"You may test these specific assets, under these rules; if you find a qualifying vulnerability and report it to us privately, we'll reward you."*

```text
Company publishes program (scope + rules + rewards)
          │
          ▼
Hunter tests in-scope assets  ──►  finds a vulnerability
          │
          ▼
Private report  ──►  Triage (is it valid? in scope? duplicate?)
          │
          ▼
Company fixes it  ──►  Reward (money, swag, points, or thanks)
          │
          ▼
Optional public disclosure (if the program allows it)
```

You're paid for **results, not hours**. A valid critical found in 20 minutes pays the same as one found in 20 days — and 20 days with no valid finding pays nothing. That single fact shapes everything about how successful hunters work.

---

## Three Things People Confuse

| | **Bug Bounty Program (BBP)** | **Vulnerability Disclosure Program (VDP)** | **Penetration Test** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who tests | Anyone who joins (public) or invited hunters (private) | Anyone | A hired, contracted team |
| Payment | Per valid bug, by severity | Usually none — thanks, points, swag | Fixed fee for the engagement |
| Duplicates | Only the first reporter is rewarded | Same | Not applicable |
| Coverage | Wherever hunters choose to look | Same | A defined scope, tested systematically |
| Good for beginners? | Yes, but competitive | **Yes — great for first findings and reputation** | Requires employment/contract |

<Admonition type="tip" title="Beginner lane">

Don't ignore VDPs. They have less competition, they still teach you real-world hunting, and valid reports there build the **reputation and signal** that get you invited to private paid programs.

</Admonition>

## Public vs Private Programs

- **Public programs** are visible to everyone on the platform. More hunters → more duplicates, especially on easy bugs.
- **Private programs** are invite-only. Platforms invite hunters based on track record (valid reports, reputation, signal/accuracy). Fewer hunters → better odds.
- **Live hacking events** are time-boxed, invite-only competitions, often in person, with boosted rewards — usually for consistent, high-performing hunters.

Your early goal isn't to get rich on public programs. It's to build a track record that gets you into private ones.

## Anatomy of a Program Policy

Read the whole policy before you send a single request. The important parts:

| Section | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **In scope** | Exact domains, wildcards (`*.example.com`), apps, API hosts, mobile app IDs, source repos |
| **Out of scope** | Domains and assets you must not touch — often third-party services, corporate sites, acquisitions |
| **Out-of-scope vulnerabilities** | Bug types they won't pay for — commonly self-XSS, missing headers, clickjacking on non-sensitive pages, rate limiting without impact, best-practice issues |
| **Rules of engagement** | Request-rate limits, required headers (e.g. a hunter identifier), required test-account email patterns, banned tools or techniques |
| **Rewards table** | Payout ranges per severity, sometimes per asset tier |
| **Severity method** | CVSS, the platform's taxonomy (for example Bugcrowd's VRT), or the company's own rating |
| **Disclosure policy** | Whether and when you may publish a write-up |
| **Safe harbor** | The company's commitment not to pursue legal action for good-faith testing within the rules |

<Admonition type="warning" title="Scope is a legal boundary">

"It's a subdomain of their main domain" does **not** make something in scope. If it isn't listed, don't test it. When unsure, ask the program through the platform before testing.

</Admonition>

## Safe Harbor and the Law

Unauthorised access to computer systems is a crime in most countries (for example under the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the UK Computer Misuse Act, or India's IT Act). A bug bounty program is what turns your testing into **authorised** testing — but only within its scope and rules.

**Safe harbor** language commits the company to treat good-faith research under the policy as authorised and not to take legal action. Many programs adopt standard safe-harbor wording from initiatives such as disclose.io. Prefer programs that include it.

You lose that protection if you:

- test out-of-scope assets,
- access, download or keep more user data than needed to prove impact,
- degrade service (DoS, heavy automated scanning against rules),
- use social engineering or physical attacks unless explicitly allowed,
- disclose publicly before the program permits it,
- try to extort ("pay me or I publish") — that's a crime, not a bounty.

## How Severity and Rewards Work

Rewards scale with **impact**, not with how clever the technique was.

| Severity | Typical shape of impact |
|---|---|
| **Critical** | Remote code execution, full account takeover of any user without interaction, mass data exposure, admin takeover |
| **High** | Account takeover with some interaction, IDOR exposing sensitive data, stored XSS on sensitive pages, SSRF to internal services |
| **Medium** | CSRF on meaningful actions, reflected XSS, limited data exposure, IDOR on less sensitive data |
| **Low** | Minor information disclosure, open redirect without chain, low-impact misconfigurations |
| **Informative / N/A** | Valid observation but no real security impact, or explicitly excluded |

Programs usually rate with **CVSS** (v3.1 or v4.0) or a platform taxonomy such as **Bugcrowd's Vulnerability Rating Taxonomy (VRT)**, then map severity to their reward table.

<Admonition type="info" title="Pro lane">

Two bugs of the same class can land at completely different severities. An IDOR on a "favourite colour" field is low; the same IDOR on bank statements is high or critical. Always ask "what can an attacker *actually do* with this, to *whom*, at *what scale*?" — and say it clearly in the report.

</Admonition>

## Duplicates: The Rule That Shapes Strategy

Only the **first valid report** of an issue gets rewarded. Later reports are closed as **duplicates** (sometimes with a small reputation bonus, never the bounty).

That's why:

- easy bugs on popular public programs are almost always duplicates,
- **depth** (understanding one app better than anyone) beats **breadth** (spraying payloads everywhere) for most hunters,
- **new features, new assets and acquisitions** are valuable — nobody has looked at them yet.

## What the Work Actually Feels Like

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Run a scanner, collect money." | Scanner-found bugs are usually found by hundreds of other people first. |
| "Hackers type fast in dark rooms." | Most time is spent *reading*: requests, JavaScript, documentation, other people's write-ups. |
| "One big bug and you're set." | Income is lumpy. Weeks of nothing, then several findings at once. |
| "You need to be a genius." | You need patience, curiosity, note-taking and persistence. |

A typical session: pick a feature, read every request it makes in your proxy, form hypotheses ("what if this ID belonged to someone else?"), test them one by one, take notes, move on. Repeat.

## Ethics in Practice

- Use **your own accounts** (create two or more) for testing.
- If you accidentally access real user data, stop, don't keep it, and mention it in your report.
- Don't run high-volume automation unless the program allows it.
- Don't chase a bug into other users' data "to show more impact." Prove it with your own accounts.
- Respect embargoes and disclosure timelines.
- Be professional with triagers, even when you disagree.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Bug bounty pays for valid, first-reported, in-scope vulnerabilities — results, not effort.
- VDPs and private programs are the beginner's best friends.
- The program policy is your contract: scope, rules, excluded bug types and safe harbor.
- Severity follows real-world impact. Duplicates follow speed and depth.
